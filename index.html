Before you start

What this is

Structure and hierarchy only. Nothing here is a colour, type or spacing decision — grey means "content goes here", not "make it grey".

Copy status

The headline, the pricing lines and "no result, no credit spent" are settled wording — they were argued over and each one covers a specific edge case. There are no grey bars left: every line on the page is now written. Two answers in §4 are still waiting on legal, which is marked in that margin.

Two layouts

Web and mobile, one breakpoint at 720px. There's no view toggle — resize the window and the wireframe reflows exactly the way the page will. Everything needs designing twice, and only twice.

Two hard rules

The hero demo works on mobile with one thumb. And the page loads fast on mobile data — no video, no Lottie, no scroll libraries. Where polish and load time conflict, take the load time.

Motion budget

Three moving things on the whole page, no more. Motion is welcome and it's a polish pass, not a design pass — full direction in the panel at the end of this document.

States to design

Every data-driven block needs four: loading, populated, empty, failed. The feed and the reveal are the two that will actually hit all four in production.

Hardest object

The lead card. It appears in the hero, in Slack, in research results and in the closing CTA. Design it once, properly, and the rest of the page follows.

The hound

Leadhoundy — the mascot is a tracking dog, and the metaphor is already exact: it picks up a scent and finds who's there. It belongs in the mark, in empty and loading states, as the Slack bot avatar, and in the footer. It does not belong in the hero, on a lead card, or anywhere in pricing.

Tone

Personality in the margins, plain language in the machinery. "Reveal" stays "Reveal" — never "Fetch". Agencies are spending $99 a week here; the dog should feel like a good logo, not like a mascot doing bits.

Why

The headline is settled and the A/B is over. Two variants used to be switchable from a control above the document; the page now ships one headline and the switcher is gone. Worth keeping in mind that the old variant B ("Every job posting is a client") repeated the subhead's claim — the current pair doesn't. The headline states the supply, the subhead states the gap, and neither says the other's sentence.

The counter line is gone because the headline swallowed it. "3,412 postings scanned today" sitting above a headline that opens "Today 3,412 companies posted work" is the same figure printed twice, and it reads as a copy-paste error rather than as proof. The number kept its live tick and moved into the sentence, so the proof mechanism survives — it just isn't a separate line any more.

The subhead now does the work the old one skipped. "Job boards don't show you who's hiring. We do." asserted the gap; this one describes it — you can see the work and the budget, you can't see who's asking — which is a thing the visitor can check against their own experience in the second it takes to read.

"The people to talk to" rather than "contacts", again. Same reason as everywhere else: at a 50/50 person-to-company split, a promise of a specific individual fails half the time, and this is the most-read sentence on the page to get that wrong in.

The pulsing dot went with the counter line, and that's a motion-budget win. It was a fourth infinite animation on a page whose own motion panel says nothing loops forever except the keyword cycle. If a live indicator ever comes back, it counts against the budget of three and something else has to give.

The whole page rests here. They see the product working on their own niche before reading a word. Everything below is support.

Keyword cycles on load so the feed is never blank. Cycling stops permanently on first click or keypress — typing into a moving field feels broken.

Three states, not two: cycling, held, stopped. Hovering the feed holds the cycle and leaving it resumes; clicking anything stops it for good. Reading a card is not the same act as choosing a keyword, so it shouldn't have the same consequence — a visitor who leans in to read one posting and then loses interest should get the rotation back, not a frozen field.

Anything a visitor does to the feed has to stop the cycle. It once didn't: 2.6 seconds after someone revealed a card, the next tick re-rendered the feed and took the result and the signup gate with it — the conversion moment on this page, deleted by a timer. That is the failure mode to watch for whenever a control is added to a card, and it is silent, so it only shows up if someone waits three seconds after clicking.

The caret carries all three states, so nothing new had to be invented: blinking means cycling, still means held while you read, gone means stopped for good. It's already the element a visitor reads for "is this thing moving", and it costs no extra furniture.

Dropdown is curated to keywords with real daily volume. Volume shown next to each: it doubles as proof the pipeline is full.

Cards are mixed on purpose. The default three cover a person, a company and a third of either kind. No badge, no label. The demo sets expectations so the first paid reveal can't feel like a bait-and-switch — which means the ordering of those first three is a real decision, not a query result. Pin it.

Hover and click do different jobs, on purpose. Hover proves an answer exists — that's curiosity, and it costs nothing. The click asks for the account. They used to be one gesture: a single click appended a profiles block and a signup gate, so the visitor hit a wall in the same beat they got their first piece of proof, and the card grew and shoved the page down as it did it.

The first name is free, and that reorders the whole pitch. Reveals cost essentially nothing to perform, so one of them is cheap advertising — and it means the visitor is holding a real result before anything is asked of them. The ask then lands on someone who already believes the product works, instead of on someone still deciding whether it's real. That is a different conversation.

One free name per visitor, not per card. Tracked on the session and never on the button — per-card scarcity means a feed of twelve cards is twelve free names, which is the whole product given away. The scarcity has to belong to the person.

The free-name counter has to live on the server. It's a variable here because this is a wireframe; in production a client-side flag is a free-name faucet that resets on reload, in a private window, or in devtools. Anyone who notices gets the entire feed for nothing, and the people most likely to notice are the ones you least want reading it for free.

An inline panel, never a modal. The dismissal reflex is about the container, not the contents — people close modals before reading them, and this one has something worth reading. Sitting under the card, it reads as the next step in what they were doing rather than as an interruption of it.

Shown once, then never again. Dismiss it and later clicks just sharpen cards silently. Showing the same panel five times is nagging, and nagging costs more than the fifth impression earns; the persistent ask lives in the hero footer, where it isn't in anyone's way.

Google is the primary button and email is a link. Two equal buttons is a decision, and a decision is friction at the exact moment you've earned the least right to ask for one.

Sharpening is one-way. Once a card is sharp it stays sharp. Re-blurring something the visitor already looked at takes the proof back and reads as the page fighting them — the state is called "found", and found doesn't get un-found.

Mobile skips the curiosity beat entirely. No hover, so cards start masked and the very next act is a click. That's a materially different funnel from desktop's hover-then-click, and averaging the two will hide whichever one is broken. Watch them separately.

The masked slot is reserved in the collapsed card. Same rows, same spacing, same strings in both states — the only thing that changes between default and hover is a blur filter. Layout stability is the entire reason for this structure, and it is measurable: the card and the feed are identical to the pixel in both states. Any future addition to a card has to be present in both states or it breaks the whole idea.

Three visual treatments, and they must stay distinct. Blur means nothing is known yet. Character masking means found and deliberately withheld. Full text means logged in. Blur reads as "still loading" and masking reads as "withheld" — using one treatment for both loses the difference, and the difference is what makes the second state feel like proof rather than a spinner.

Only the masked string is ever rendered. The real name is not in the card's markup at any point, so it can't be read out of devtools, and a blurred screenshot can't be sharpened back into it. CSS blur is a visual effect, not a redaction — if production ever sends the real name and blurs it, the paywall is decorative. Mask on the server, not in the stylesheet.

Mobile starts masked and never transitions. There is no hover on a phone and roughly half your traffic taps, so the compelling state is simply the default one there — a transition needs a "before", and on mobile there isn't one.

The panel names the posting the visitor was already looking at. A generic signup form is an interruption; "get the name behind Migrate Framer site to Webflow" is the continuation of a task already in progress. This is also the strongest argument for making the keyword carry through signup — if it does, the account they land in is already the one they were imagining.

The button says "Get the name" now, not "Reveal". It matches what the headline promises two lines above it. "Reveal" stays everywhere it means the billing unit — 7 free reveals, 150 reveals a week — because that is a unit of account, not a description of what the visitor receives.

"The free reveals are the reward for signing up, not something spent on the landing page" — that rule now has one exception, deliberately. Exactly one name is spent before signup. The 7 still belong to the account; this is a sample, not a draw against them, which is why the card says "on us" rather than counting down.

Reveal is a partial, not a giveaway. Any card can be revealed, any number of times. The blur comes off and the answer's shape appears — individual or company, how many profiles matched, their job titles — but the names stay masked. They learn the answer exists and that it's specific. The 7 free reveals are the reward for signing up, not something spent on the landing page.

Currency is USD throughout, since that's what the source platforms quote regardless of where the client sits. Don't convert to local currency — it adds a rounding artifact and makes the number look calculated rather than copied.

Job titles are the part worth showing. "Head of Growth" and "Operations Lead" prove the match is real in a way a blurred name never could, and they're the reason someone believes the next reveal is worth an account.

Custom keywords fall back to historical postings — and the label changes to "Recent matches". Never label stale results "Live now".

Paste-a-link demoted to a quiet line. Kept for the Upwork user with a tab already open.

Build notes

  1. 0 Wordmark plus hound mark. This is the mascot's most important placement — it has to work at 24px in a browser tab and as a Slack bot avatar, so the dog needs to survive being tiny. Design the avatar crop first and let the full mark follow.
  2. 1 Only filled button above the fold. Nav is otherwise quiet — every unit of attention above the feed belongs to the keyword field.
  3. 13 Three nav links, and four is the ceiling. How it works · Pricing · FAQ — one per section a visitor might actually jump to, in page order. It was four until Ways in was folded into How it works; a link for it now would land on the same section, and two labels pointing at one destination is worse than one label. Deliberately not a link per section either: Hero, Contrast and Close aren't destinations, they're the argument. Every one of these is a way out of the demo, so they stay at hint weight — grey, no underline, no hover lift. If a fourth gets proposed, it has to earn its place.
  4. 2 Live, or gone — and it's now inside the headline. There is no separate counter line any more; the number in the first sentence is the live one and it ticks on its own. If the count fails to load, the headline still has to read correctly, so it needs a defensible fallback figure rather than a spinner or a gap. A frozen counter is worse than no counter, and a frozen counter inside a headline is worse still. Never round to a fake milestone.
  5. 2 Set the number in tabular figures. It changes every four seconds inside a line of running text, and proportional digits make the words after it shuffle sideways on each tick. This is the one place on the page where that detail is load-bearing rather than a nicety.
  6. 3 Copy is locked, and there is only one version now. The A/B switcher is gone — this headline is the headline. "Their names" is true whether the reveal returns a person or a company, which is the constraint every rewrite has to clear. Don't rewrite to "get their email" or "find the decision maker": the first is a channel we don't deliver, the second breaks on half our results.
  7. 12 Popular tags are the one-tap path. The dropdown costs two clicks — open, then choose. These cost one, and they double as proof that real volume exists across services. The active tag stays in sync with the field, including while it cycles, so a visitor always sees which keyword the feed belongs to.
  8. 4 Label is a sentence, not a field name. "Show me clients looking for [Webflow]" explains the input and the output in one read, so no separate instruction is needed. Never the word "niche" — half your audience would read that as an industry (dental, legal) and type something with no volume behind it. The nouns are services and technologies.
  9. 4 Mobile breaks the sentence. An inline field inside a sentence wraps badly on a phone. The label stacks above the field at narrow widths — narrow the window past 720px to check it.
  10. 4 Must read as editable, not as a dropdown. It's a text field with suggestions. Cycling keyword stops permanently on first click or keypress, and the caret disappears with it. On mobile this is the one thing a thumb has to find instantly.
  11. 5 The hold is deliberately silent. No "paused" badge, no overlay, no icon. A visitor who hovers a card to read it has not asked for a status report, and a carousel that announces its own pausing draws attention to the fact that it was moving. The caret going still is the entire cue, and most people will never consciously notice it — which is the point.
  12. 5 Hover is bound only where hovering is real. Behind a (hover: hover) check, because on a touch screen mouseenter fires on tap and no leave arrives until the visitor taps somewhere else — the cycle would look dead. Touch doesn't need it anyway: a tap on Reveal is a click, and a click stops the cycle outright.
  13. 5 Keyboard gets the same courtesy. Tabbing onto a Reveal button holds the cycle exactly as hovering does; the field is the same intent expressed with a different device. Without it, a keyboard visitor reading the feed watches it re-render underneath their focus ring.
  14. 7 Any action on a card ends the cycle, not just the keyword field. Get the name, and anything added to a card later. The rule to hold on to: if a visitor changed the state of the feed, the feed is theirs and a timer must never take it back.
  15. 5 Two label states. "Live now / last 24h" for curated keywords; "Recent matches for X / from the archive" for custom ones. Design both — the second is not an error state, it's normal.
  16. 6 Budget is the credibility anchor. A real number does more than any copy, and it stays sharp in every state — give it visual weight above the tags.
  17. 6 Two budget formats only. Fixed price ($4,500) or hourly rate ($55/h) — nothing else. Fixed runs to five digits, hourly to two, so the slot must hold both without the row reflowing. Postings with no budget will still happen: decide now whether the card says "budget not stated" or drops the chip, because an empty slot breaks the row.
  18. 7 Two button states, and the second one is quiet. "Get the name" is the only interactive state; after the free name lands the button becomes "Got it" — non-interactive, low contrast, still occupying the same slot so nothing moves. It also needs a loading state, because the lookup isn't instant in production and this is the one press where the visitor is waiting on a real answer.
  19. 7 A gated click is never a dead press. Even after the panel has been dismissed, clicking still pins the card to the masked state. A button that visibly does nothing reads as broken; a button that does something small reads as a boundary.
  20. 14 Nothing may change height between the two states. Name and role are single-line with overflow hidden, and both hold the masked string in both states, so only the blur changes. Verify this every time the card gains anything: hover a card and watch the cards below it — if they move a pixel, the change is wrong.
  21. 14 Three treatments, never interchangeable. Blur = nothing is known yet. Character mask = found and withheld. Full = delivered. Blur reads as "still loading" and masking reads as "deliberately withheld" — one treatment for both loses the difference, and the difference is what makes the middle state feel like proof instead of a spinner. Design all three; the middle one is the one that sells.
  22. 14 The full state has to look like a different kind of thing. Back to the sans face, no hatch on the avatar, no monospace. If the delivered name still carries any of the withheld state's styling, the visitor won't be sure they actually got it — and this is the single moment the whole page is built to produce.
  23. 14 Sharpening is one-way, and it's JavaScript that makes it stick. CSS drives the first hover so the response is instant and survives the script not having run; a listener then pins the state so it never reverts. Don't collapse this into CSS alone — :hover by itself takes the proof back the moment the pointer leaves.
  24. 14 Job titles stay unmasked at every stage. "Head of Growth" is what proves the match is real — a blurred name proves nothing. The company is masked on a person result and left readable on a company one, because on a company result the organisation is the answer, not the secret.
  25. 14 The transition is ~200ms and only on the blur. Fast enough to read as caused, slow enough not to look like a glitch. The card border shifts colour on the same curve so the content change has a visible cause; the border width must never change or the layout moves.
  26. 14 The profile count is plain text and never blurs. "2 profiles matched" is the one piece of the answer given away for free in every state, and it's what makes the blurred rows above it read as withheld rather than empty. It also carries the person-versus-company split without a badge.
  27. 15 The panel expands below the card, and it is the only thing on this card allowed to add height. Everything above it holds still, so the page grows downward from a fixed point rather than rearranging around the visitor. Never a modal — see the margin.
  28. 15 The posting title is the whole point of the panel. It can be long, so let it wrap to two or three lines rather than truncating; the specificity is the argument and an ellipsis throws it away. It gets its own line under the sentence for the same reason.
  29. 15 One button and one link, not two buttons. Google carries the weight; email is a quiet text link that reads as an escape hatch rather than an alternative. If email ever becomes a second filled button, the panel has stopped being an invitation and started being a form.
  30. 15 Dismissal is permanent and needs no confirmation. One close control, top right, and the panel never comes back in that session. Design the dismissed state as the normal path — most people will take it, and the ones who don't were always going to sign up.
  31. 16 This line has to be read before the first click, or it does nothing. Its job is to make the click feel free and the later gate feel fair rather than sudden. Test its placement — under three cards it may sit below where people stop scanning, and if it does, it belongs above them instead. It disappears once the free name is spent, because it stops being true.
  32. 8 Three phrasings of the same promise now sit within one screen. This foot line says "7 free reveals", the card says "7 more", the panel says "7 names included". Same number, three nouns. Reveals are the billing unit and names are what the visitor receives, so the visitor-facing copy should say names everywhere and leave "reveals" to pricing — which would make this line "7 free names when you sign up". Worth settling before the page ships; it is the kind of drift nobody notices until a customer quotes it back.
  33. 11 Mobile nav collapses to a burger and the CTA stays out of it. "Start free" never hides behind a menu — it's the only thing in the nav that converts. With four links rather than two, the burger is now carrying real content: it needs a designed open state, not just an icon.
  34. — Mobile: keyword field goes full width, directly under the subheadline. Reveal buttons go full width too. Every tap target is at least 44px and spans the card, because on a phone the demo is the entire page.
  35. 10 The Load more button was cut deliberately. It put a second control next to "Get the name" and competed with it, it pushed pricing further down the page, and it answered a question nobody was asking — a visitor's next thought after three cards is what's the name?, not are there more?
  36. 10 The line argues better than more cards would. It says the supply is bigger than what's shown without costing a scroll or an interaction, which is the same claim the button was making at a much higher price.
  37. 10 Three cards also protect the free name. One free out of three reads as generous; one free out of twelve reads as stingy. The size of the feed sets how big the gift feels, so growing it costs something that isn't obvious from the feed alone.
  38. 10 Quiet line, not a control. Same weight as the hint text around it, no border, no background, no hover state. The moment it acquires an affordance it becomes the button again, and people will click it and find nothing happens.
  39. 10 The number is real arithmetic, not decoration. Daily volume minus the three on screen, and it moves with the keyword — including mid-cycle. Volumes are read off the dropdown rather than kept in a second list, so the two can't drift; if a volume changes there, this line follows it. A keyword with no known volume never gets an invented number, it gets the archive sentence instead.
  40. 10 If analytics later show people scrolling past the hero untouched, this is where a low-commitment control goes back in. Clicking anything makes the next click likelier. Don't add it pre-emptively — the point of removing it was to find out whether the demo can carry the section on its own.
  41. 9 Deliberately quiet. This is a shortcut for people who arrive with a link, not a second front door. If it starts competing with the keyword field, shrink it.
  42. — No hound above the fold. The hero has one job and a mascot competing with the feed costs conversions. The exception is the loading state while a reveal resolves — a dog on a scent is exactly the right thing to look at while you wait, and it's the moment the metaphor pays off.

Why

The density difference registers before a word is read. That is the section: fifteen dots crowding one client against a single line reaching one client. Eight bullets was roughly fifteen seconds of reading and mental diffing for a section budgeted at four, and a section that takes fifteen seconds in a four-second slot doesn't get read slowly — it gets skipped.

The headings are now structurally identical. "They pick you." / "You pick them." — same shape, same length, one swapped word, so the brain diffs them automatically instead of parsing two different sentences. The old pair was passive against active, which was thematically right and comparatively slow.

The specifics were dropped on purpose. Connects, the platform cut, being late to the posting — all gone. §2's job is to make someone feel the difference at a glance; specifics belong in §3 and the FAQ, where people are already reading and willing to. A section trying to do both does neither, and this one was doing the feeling badly.

Still reads correctly for both audiences: the Upwork agency recognises the crowd immediately, and the half who have never touched a job board understand "they pick you" without needing to know what a proposal queue is. The venue stays on the kicker, where the hero subhead already put it.

Resist expanding this back into a problem/solution section. It's a strip, and it is now the length a strip should be.

Build notes

  1. 1 Don't make the left side ugly. Grey it, don't villainise it — many visitors still work that way today and a sneering panel insults them. The asymmetry comes from density, not from red crosses and green ticks. Nothing on the left is broken; there is simply a lot of it.
  2. 4 The visual has to work with the headings covered up. That's the test: hide both headings and both captions, and the two pictures alone should still say crowd against direct line. If they don't, the dots aren't doing their job and no amount of caption will rescue them.
  3. 4 Fifteen dots is a representation, not a count. The caption says forty; the picture says "a lot". Don't try to draw thirty-nine — past about twenty the shape stops reading as a crowd and starts reading as noise, and the number belongs in the caption where it can be exact.
  4. 4 Worth trying: one dot in the crowd is you. The caption already says "you and 39 others", so marking a single dot in the accent would land the point harder. It is not drawn that way here because it puts accent colour on the left side, which is the one thing the colour rule below forbids — so it needs deciding, not defaulting.
  5. 5 Colour is meaningful here and only here. Left neutral, right accent, because on this one strip the colour is the argument rather than a treatment applied to it. Everywhere else on the page colour is either ink or annotation. If a brand accent gets introduced anywhere first, introduce it here.
  6. 5 The accent is a token, not a colour decision. --accent resolves to ink today so this document stays two-tone and honest about being a wireframe. Swap that one variable and the section takes the real brand colour; nothing else needs touching, and nothing else should be.
  7. 5 Drawn, not placed. Inline SVG, circles and thin lines, no image request and no icon font — a few hundred bytes, sharp at any size, and it inherits its colour from CSS so the two sides differ by one property. It also means the density can be tuned in the markup rather than re-exported.
  8. 5 Both sides share one viewBox. Same coordinate space, same client shape in the same position, so the only thing that differs between the two pictures is what's on the left of it. Change one and change the other, or the comparison quietly stops being fair.
  9. 4 On mobile the columns stack and both drawings get narrower. The density contrast is the thing to check there — the crowd may shed dots, but if it stops reading as a crowd the section has lost its argument at the width where most people meet it.
  10. 2 The arrow carries the whole idea. On mobile the columns stack and it rotates to point down, which keeps the logic but is doing it with a rotated character — the weakest element in the section at the width where most people will read it. Design the vertical state properly, or the strip reads as two unrelated lists on a phone.
  11. 2 Worth trying: the arrow is the hound. The mark is literally the thing that moves you from the left column to the right — picks up a scent, finds who's there. It's the one place outside the mark, the empty states and the footer where the dog would be doing work rather than decorating. Try it, but only after the vertical stacked state above is solved, because a brand riding on the weakest element in the section is worse than a plain arrow.
  12. 3 Two kickers, one job each. They name the route so the headers are free to name the behaviour. Same size, same weight, same position in both columns — the moment the right kicker gets styled as a badge or a logo lockup, the strip stops being an observation and starts being an ad, and the left column reads as a straw man. Quiet mono label, nothing more.
  13. 3 Headers must sit on the same baseline. The kicker exists on both columns partly so they do. Any state where one column has a kicker and the other doesn't breaks the mirror, which is the only reason this section works.
  14. — One specific is worth reinstating if this feels too thin in testing: "no proposals, no bidding, no cut". It justifies the price and currently appears only in step 4 of §3. It would go under the right-hand caption, as one line — and if it needs two, it belongs in §3 after all.

Why

Scanner is the spine of the page — the hero demo and these steps — not one of several equal features. Everything in the "Also included" strip exists to close a "but what if I want X" loop, not to be chosen.

Step 4 keeps "no proposal queue, no bidding, no cut". That line was dropped from the hero subhead when it was shortened. If it isn't here it is nowhere on the page, and it is the sentence that explains why this is worth $99 a week.

Steps 1 and 2 are both configuration, which risks reading as more setup than the one-tap hero implied. "About a minute" defuses that, and it is the only reason the pair survives being adjacent.

Research can't be cut. Archive depth — 7 days, 45 days, full — is a row in the pricing table, and an unexplained pricing row is worse than a small section.

Browser extension stays off the page until it ships. A section that leads nowhere costs more trust than the feature earns.

Objections — five, folded except the first6

What exactly do I get for a credit?1

The person or company behind the posting, plus matching LinkedIn profiles. Sometimes we can name the individual directly, sometimes the company and the people to talk to there.

Where does this data come from?2

Public job boards — the places companies post paid project work. Everything we use is published openly: the posting itself, and the professional profiles we match it to. We don't buy lists, and we don't touch anything private.

Can I get banned for contacting people directly?4

We can't speak for another platform's rules — if you're active on one, read its terms and use your judgment. What we give you is who published a posting and their public professional profile. Where you take it from there is your call, and it happens outside that platform.

What if a reveal finds nothing?3

The credit isn't taken. You only spend one when we return a result.

Does cold outreach actually work on these clients?5

Yes, but they're leads, not guaranteed clients. These are companies with a budget and stated intent, so reply rates are far better than untargeted cold email. Some buy, most don't — same as any pipeline.

What decides it is the pitch. Agencies getting the most out of this pick one thing they're excellent at and reach out only where they're clearly the best answer. A generic proposal sent to a well-targeted lead is still a generic proposal.

Why

Row 1 is the 50/50 disclosure. Written as a spec, not an apology. It belongs here, never in the hero — the mixed demo feed does the expectation-setting up top.

Row 4 is a conversion lever, not an FAQ answer. It also appears next to pricing.

This section used to be open with no accordions, and that was a deliberate rule. The old note read: "a collapsed objection is an unanswered one, and the whole point is that these are read without being clicked." It was overruled on space — five open answers ran 625px on desktop and 872px on a phone, which is most of a viewport of prose sitting between the product and the price. The fold is the concession; which row stays open is how the original concern gets paid for.

Row 1 stays open because nobody would click it. It's the 50/50 disclosure — a visitor doesn't arrive wondering whether they'll get a person or a company, so they'd never open it, and it's the one answer that stops the first paid reveal feeling like a bait-and-switch. Folding it would have been the one genuinely costly fold on the page.

Rows 2, 3 and 5 fold safely because they answer questions a visitor actually has. Someone worried about the data source, a ban, or whether cold outreach works arrives already holding that question; a visible question with an obvious affordance gets clicked. The question text does most of the reassurance work anyway — seeing "Can I get banned for contacting people directly?" printed on the page tells a careful buyer the concern is acknowledged before they read a word of the answer.

Row 4 folds for free. Its answer is deliberately duplicated next to pricing, so a visitor who never opens it here still meets "no result, no credit spent" further down. It's the only row on the page with that property, which is exactly why it can afford to be closed.

All five answers are now written — these were the last grey bars on the page. Rows 2 and 3 still need the lawyer before publishing, though; being written is not the same as being cleared.

Rows 2 and 3 are the two that get screenshotted. One says where the data comes from, the other says whether using it is safe. They're the reason a careful buyer either signs up or closes the tab, and they're the two most likely to be quoted back at you — so they stay plain, specific and short. No hedging language, no legal register, no "we take privacy seriously".

"Published openly" is not the same as "lawful to process". Under GDPR, personal data that someone published is still personal data, and row 2 doesn't settle the posture — it describes the sourcing honestly, which is the right thing for a landing page to do and not a substitute for the legal review. If counsel needs a sentence added, it goes here rather than into a policy nobody opens.

"Reply rates are far better than untargeted cold email" is a performance claim. Not comparative against a named company, so it doesn't carry the exposure the headline rules were guarding against — but it's still a claim you'd need to stand behind. Either get a number from the two customers and say it, or soften "far" before this ships.

Row 3's real defence is its last clause — the outreach happens somewhere else entirely. That's the load-bearing sentence in the answer, and it should survive any edit.

  2. 6 The affordance has to be unmistakable, because the fold only works if people click. A row that looks like static text is a lost answer.   3. 6 Built on native <details>, and it should stay that way. No JavaScript, keyboard-operable and screen-reader-announced for free, and it works before the script runs — which is a rule on this page.   4. 6 Multiple rows can be open at once, deliberately. An exclusive accordion (which <details name> would give free) would slam row 1 shut the moment someone opens row 2, and row 1 is the one that must stay readable. It also stops anyone comparing two answers.
  5. 6 Expanding must not animate. Height transitions on open are the single most common accordion tell, and this page has a three-item motion budget that this is not on. Instant open, no easing, nothing to respect prefers-reduced-motion about.
  6. 6 Never collapse a row that's carrying a legal answer once counsel has signed it off.   7. 2 Flat, unbothered tone. These read as spec, not as reassurance. No warning icons, no tinted "trust" panel, no shield graphics — defensive design makes the answers look defensive.
  8. 3 This row appears twice. Here, and next to pricing. Same wording both times; it's a conversion lever, not an FAQ answer. If the copy drifts between the two, the guarantee stops sounding like a rule.

  10. 5 Two paragraphs, and the second one is the useful half. The first sets expectations — leads, not clients. The second tells them how to actually win, which is the paragraph that earns trust, because a page that admits a generic pitch will fail is a page that isn't selling magic. Last step — motion, feel, and the speed ceiling

Sequence: motion is a polish pass

Design the static page and all four states first — card, type scale, spacing system. Only then spend the motion budget.

If the static page needs animation to feel good, the animation is compensating for something, and the page will feel expensive for two seconds and thin afterwards.

The speed ceiling comes first

This page has to load fast on a phone on mobile data. Speed is not a constraint on the premium feel — for an infrastructure product it is the premium feel.

  • No video, no Lottie, no WebGL, no scroll libraries
  • Hound as SVG, not a raster illustration
  • Two font weights maximum, self-hosted and subset
  • Animate transform and opacity only — nothing that triggers layout
  • If a decision trades load time for polish, take the load time

Where premium actually comes from

Not from effects. From typography with real weight range, a two-colour palette used confidently, a strict spacing scale, and craft in the small things — designed focus rings, optical alignment, tabular figures so the counter doesn't jitter.

Density done calmly. Cheap B2B is sparse and shouty; expensive B2B is dense and quiet. One elevated surface — the lead card — and everything else flat.

The hound has to be a crest, not a cartoon

A mascot and a $99/week price pull against each other. Draw it like a heraldic mark or an old hunting-club emblem: confident line, no big eyes, no smile, no speech bubbles. If it reads as a sticker, it costs more credibility than it buys warmth.

Motion budget — three things, total

  • Keyword cycle. Mechanical and confident. Hard swap or fast crossfade, never a slide-up. Holds while the pointer or focus is in the feed, and stops permanently on first interaction.
  • The reveal. The product's whole thesis in one interaction — this gets the craft. Mask lifts, card expands, content below moves aside. Under 400ms end to end. The hound-on-a-scent loading state lives here and nowhere else.
  • New lead arriving. One card slides in at the top while they watch. Proves "live" better than any label. Rate-limit hard — one arrival per ~20s, not a stream.

Explicitly ruled out

  • Scroll-triggered section entrances
  • Counters that animate up on scroll
  • Parallax of any kind
  • Card hover lifts and shadow transitions
  • Animated gradient backgrounds, cursor followers

Each of these ships in every template. Using them is how a page announces where it came from. And all of them cost load time for nothing.

Non-negotiables

  • Everything respects prefers-reduced-motion
  • Nothing loops forever except the keyword cycle, which holds on hover and stops on interaction
  • No animation blocks the demo — a visitor can type a keyword and hit Reveal before any of it finishes
  • The page is usable and legible before JavaScript runs
