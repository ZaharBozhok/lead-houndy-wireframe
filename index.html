Before you start

What this is

Structure and hierarchy only. Nothing here is a colour, type or spacing decision — grey means "content goes here", not "make it grey".

Copy status

The headline, the pricing lines and "no result, no credit spent" are settled wording — they were argued over and each one covers a specific edge case. There are no grey bars left: every line on the page is now written. Two answers in §4 are still waiting on legal, which is marked in that margin.

Two layouts

Web and mobile, one breakpoint at 720px. There's no view toggle — resize the window and the wireframe reflows exactly the way the page will. Everything needs designing twice, and only twice.

Two hard rules

The hero demo works on mobile with one thumb. And the page loads fast on mobile data — no video, no Lottie, no scroll libraries. Where polish and load time conflict, take the load time.

Motion budget

Three moving things on the whole page, no more. Motion is welcome and it's a polish pass, not a design pass — full direction in the panel at the end of this document.

States to design

Every data-driven block needs four: loading, populated, empty, failed. The feed and the reveal are the two that will actually hit all four in production.

Hardest object

The lead card. It appears in the hero, in Slack, in research results and in the closing CTA. Design it once, properly, and the rest of the page follows.

The hound

Leadhoundy — the mascot is a tracking dog, and the metaphor is already exact: it picks up a scent and finds who's there. It belongs in the mark, in empty and loading states, as the Slack bot avatar, and in the footer. It does not belong in the hero, on a lead card, or anywhere in pricing.

Tone

Personality in the margins, plain language in the machinery. "Reveal" stays "Reveal" — never "Fetch". Agencies are spending $99 a week here; the dog should feel like a good logo, not like a mascot doing bits.

Why

The headline is settled and the A/B is over. Two variants used to be switchable from a control above the document; the page now ships one headline and the switcher is gone. Worth keeping in mind that the old variant B ("Every job posting is a client") repeated the subhead's claim — the current pair doesn't. The headline states the supply, the subhead states the gap, and neither says the other's sentence.

The counter line is gone because the headline swallowed it. "3,412 postings scanned today" sitting above a headline that opens "Today 3,412 companies posted work" is the same figure printed twice, and it reads as a copy-paste error rather than as proof. The number kept its live tick and moved into the sentence, so the proof mechanism survives — it just isn't a separate line any more.

The subhead now does the work the old one skipped. "Job boards don't show you who's hiring. We do." asserted the gap; this one describes it — you can see the work and the budget, you can't see who's asking — which is a thing the visitor can check against their own experience in the second it takes to read.

"The people to talk to" rather than "contacts", again. Same reason as everywhere else: at a 50/50 person-to-company split, a promise of a specific individual fails half the time, and this is the most-read sentence on the page to get that wrong in.

The pulsing dot went with the counter line, and that's a motion-budget win. It was a fourth infinite animation on a page whose own motion panel says nothing loops forever except the keyword cycle. If a live indicator ever comes back, it counts against the budget of three and something else has to give.

The whole page rests here. They see the product working on their own niche before reading a word. Everything below is support.

Keyword cycles on load so the feed is never blank. Cycling stops permanently on first click or keypress — typing into a moving field feels broken.

Three states, not two: cycling, held, stopped. Hovering the feed holds the cycle and leaving it resumes; clicking anything stops it for good. Reading a card is not the same act as choosing a keyword, so it shouldn't have the same consequence — a visitor who leans in to read one posting and then loses interest should get the rotation back, not a frozen field.

Reveal and Load more used to be silently undone. Neither stopped the cycle, so 2.6 seconds after a visitor revealed a card the next tick re-rendered the feed and took the masked name, the matched profiles and the signup gate with it — the conversion moment on this page, deleted by a timer. Load more had the same problem: six cards dropped back to three. Both now stop the cycle, which is what "stops on first interaction" always meant.

The caret carries all three states, so nothing new had to be invented: blinking means cycling, still means held while you read, gone means stopped for good. It's already the element a visitor reads for "is this thing moving", and it costs no extra furniture.

Dropdown is curated to keywords with real daily volume. Volume shown next to each: it doubles as proof the pipeline is full.

Cards are mixed on purpose. The default three cover a person, a company and a third of either kind. No badge, no label. The demo sets expectations so the first paid reveal can't feel like a bait-and-switch — which means the ordering of those first three is a real decision, not a query result. Pin it.

Reveal is a partial, not a giveaway. Any card can be revealed, any number of times. The blur comes off and the answer's shape appears — individual or company, how many profiles matched, their job titles — but the names stay masked. They learn the answer exists and that it's specific. The 15 free reveals are the reward for signing up, not something spent on the landing page.

Currency is USD throughout, since that's what the source platforms quote regardless of where the client sits. Don't convert to local currency — it adds a rounding artifact and makes the number look calculated rather than copied.

Job titles are the part worth showing. "Head of Growth" and "Operations Lead" prove the match is real in a way a blurred name never could, and they're the reason someone believes the next reveal is worth an account.

Custom keywords fall back to historical postings — and the label changes to "Recent matches". Never label stale results "Live now".

Paste-a-link demoted to a quiet line. Kept for the Upwork user with a tab already open.

Build notes

  1. 0 Wordmark plus hound mark. This is the mascot's most important placement — it has to work at 24px in a browser tab and as a Slack bot avatar, so the dog needs to survive being tiny. Design the avatar crop first and let the full mark follow.
  2. 1 Only filled button above the fold. Nav is otherwise quiet — every unit of attention above the feed belongs to the keyword field.
  3. 13 Three nav links, and four is the ceiling. How it works · Pricing · FAQ — one per section a visitor might actually jump to, in page order. It was four until Ways in was folded into How it works; a link for it now would land on the same section, and two labels pointing at one destination is worse than one label. Deliberately not a link per section either: Hero, Contrast and Close aren't destinations, they're the argument. Every one of these is a way out of the demo, so they stay at hint weight — grey, no underline, no hover lift. If a fourth gets proposed, it has to earn its place.
  4. 2 Live, or gone — and it's now inside the headline. There is no separate counter line any more; the number in the first sentence is the live one and it ticks on its own. If the count fails to load, the headline still has to read correctly, so it needs a defensible fallback figure rather than a spinner or a gap. A frozen counter is worse than no counter, and a frozen counter inside a headline is worse still. Never round to a fake milestone.
  5. 2 Set the number in tabular figures. It changes every four seconds inside a line of running text, and proportional digits make the words after it shuffle sideways on each tick. This is the one place on the page where that detail is load-bearing rather than a nicety.
  6. 3 Copy is locked, and there is only one version now. The A/B switcher is gone — this headline is the headline. "Their names" is true whether the reveal returns a person or a company, which is the constraint every rewrite has to clear. Don't rewrite to "get their email" or "find the decision maker": the first is a channel we don't deliver, the second breaks on half our results.
  7. 12 Popular tags are the one-tap path. The dropdown costs two clicks — open, then choose. These cost one, and they double as proof that real volume exists across services. The active tag stays in sync with the field, including while it cycles, so a visitor always sees which keyword the feed belongs to.
  8. 4 Label is a sentence, not a field name. "Show me clients looking for [Webflow]" explains the input and the output in one read, so no separate instruction is needed. Never the word "niche" — half your audience would read that as an industry (dental, legal) and type something with no volume behind it. The nouns are services and technologies.
  9. 4 Mobile breaks the sentence. An inline field inside a sentence wraps badly on a phone. The label stacks above the field at narrow widths — narrow the window past 720px to check it.
  10. 4 Must read as editable, not as a dropdown. It's a text field with suggestions. Cycling keyword stops permanently on first click or keypress, and the caret disappears with it. On mobile this is the one thing a thumb has to find instantly.
  11. 5 The hold is deliberately silent. No "paused" badge, no overlay, no icon. A visitor who hovers a card to read it has not asked for a status report, and a carousel that announces its own pausing draws attention to the fact that it was moving. The caret going still is the entire cue, and most people will never consciously notice it — which is the point.
  12. 5 Hover is bound only where hovering is real. Behind a (hover: hover) check, because on a touch screen mouseenter fires on tap and no leave arrives until the visitor taps somewhere else — the cycle would look dead. Touch doesn't need it anyway: a tap on Reveal is a click, and a click stops the cycle outright.
  13. 5 Keyboard gets the same courtesy. Tabbing onto a Reveal button holds the cycle exactly as hovering does; the field is the same intent expressed with a different device. Without it, a keyboard visitor reading the feed watches it re-render underneath their focus ring.
  14. 7 Any action on a card ends the cycle, not just the keyword field. Reveal, Load more, and anything added to a card later. The rule to hold on to: if a visitor changed the state of the feed, the feed is theirs and a timer must never take it back.
  15. 5 Two label states. "Live now / last 24h" for curated keywords; "Recent matches for X / from the archive" for custom ones. Design both — the second is not an error state, it's normal.
  16. 6 Budget is the credibility anchor. A real number does more than any copy, and it stays sharp in every state — give it visual weight above the tags.
  17. 6 Two budget formats only. Fixed price ($4,500) or hourly rate ($55/h) — nothing else. Fixed runs to five digits, hourly to two, so the slot must hold both without the row reflowing. Postings with no budget will still happen: decide now whether the card says "budget not stated" or drops the chip, because an empty slot breaks the row.
  18. 7 Three button states. Reveal → Revealed (quiet, done) → Locked (gated). Plus a loading state; the lookup isn't instant in production.
  19. 8 Reveal opens in place. Never a modal, never a new page. Card grows, content below shifts down. Company reveals expand taller than person reveals — design for the tall one.
  20. 8 Three visual states, deliberately different. Blurred (nothing known yet) → masked characters (found, not legible) → full (after login). The switch from blur to character-masking matters: blur reads as "still loading", masking reads as "deliberately withheld". Don't use the same treatment for both.
  21. 11 Mobile nav collapses to a burger and the CTA stays out of it. "Start free" never hides behind a menu — it's the only thing in the nav that converts. With four links rather than two, the burger is now carrying real content: it needs a designed open state, not just an icon.
  22. — Mobile: keyword field goes full width, directly under the subheadline. Reveal buttons go full width too. Every tap target is at least 44px and spans the card, because on a phone the demo is the entire page.
  23. 10 Three cards by default, three per click. The hero has to stay above the fold on a laptop, and three is enough to show the mix — person, company, different budgets — without pushing the rest of the page below the screen. Anyone who wants more is engaged enough to ask.
  24. 10 Load more, not infinite scroll. Each click is a deliberate act; infinite scroll on a landing page fights the scroll down to pricing. The end state names Research, which turns running out into a pitch for the tier above.
  25. 9 Deliberately quiet. This is a shortcut for people who arrive with a link, not a second front door. If it starts competing with the keyword field, shrink it.
  26. — No hound above the fold. The hero has one job and a mascot competing with the feed costs conversions. The exception is the loading state while a reveal resolves — a dog on a scent is exactly the right thing to look at while you wait, and it's the moment the metaphor pays off.

Why

Reads correctly for both audiences: the Upwork agency recognises every line on the left, and the half who've never touched a job board still understand "waiting to be picked" — it's a feeling they've had somewhere else. The left column now names a behaviour rather than a venue; the venue moved down to the kicker, where it belongs, because the hero subhead already established it.

Each row is a matched pair, and the mirror is the mechanism. What your money buys, whether you're in a queue, what's left when the job closes, whether a new agency can win. Read across, not down. If a line is ever rewritten, its opposite number gets rewritten with it or the row stops working.

Left side is described, not attacked. "Waiting to be picked" is the one line here with any edge on it, and a good share of your visitors work that way today. It stays because it's accurate, not because it's cutting — and every answer on the right is warm rather than triumphant. No red crosses, no green ticks.

This strip got longer, deliberately. Roughly four seconds became seven or eight. The old version listed symptoms ("40 proposals sent"); this one makes four arguments, which is worth the extra beats. It is now at its ceiling — if anything else needs saying, it goes in §4, and if the budget has to be held, row 2 is the one to cut because row 1 already implies it.

Resist expanding this into a problem/solution section. It's still a strip.

"Connects" is gone. It's Upwork's word and invisible to half the audience; "pay more to move up" says the same thing to everyone.

Build notes

  1. 1 Don't make the left side ugly. Grey it, don't villainise it — many visitors still work that way and a sneering panel insults them. The asymmetry should come from the content, not from red crosses and green ticks.
  2. 2 The arrow carries the whole idea. On mobile the columns stack and it rotates to point down, which keeps the logic but is doing it with a rotated character — the weakest element in the section at the width where most people will read it. Design the vertical state properly, or the strip reads as two unrelated lists on a phone.
  3. 2 Worth trying: the arrow is the hound. The mark is literally the thing that moves you from the left column to the right — picks up a scent, finds who's there. It's the one place outside the mark, the empty states and the footer where the dog would be doing work rather than decorating. Try it, but only after the vertical stacked state above is solved, because a brand riding on the weakest element in the section is worse than a plain arrow.
  4. 3 Two kickers, one job each. They name the route so the headers are free to name the behaviour. Same size, same weight, same position in both columns — the moment the right kicker gets styled as a badge or a logo lockup, the strip stops being an observation and starts being an ad, and the left column reads as a straw man. Quiet mono label, nothing more.
  5. 3 Headers must sit on the same baseline. The kicker exists on both columns partly so they do. Any state where one column has a kicker and the other doesn't breaks the mirror, which is the only reason this section works.
  6. — Lines will wrap to two rows at most widths. That's expected — but the wrap point must not fall mid-phrase on "No reviews, no work. No work, no reviews", which is the one line whose rhythm carries it. Check it at the narrow end of the web layout, not just wide.

Objections — five, folded except the first6

What exactly do I get for a credit?1

The person or company behind the posting, plus matching LinkedIn profiles. Sometimes we can name the individual directly, sometimes the company and the people to talk to there.

Where does this data come from?2

Public job boards — the places companies post paid project work. Everything we use is published openly: the posting itself, and the professional profiles we match it to. We don't buy lists, and we don't touch anything private.

Can I get banned for contacting people directly?4

We can't speak for another platform's rules — if you're active on one, read its terms and use your judgment. What we give you is who published a posting and their public professional profile. Where you take it from there is your call, and it happens outside that platform.

What if a reveal finds nothing?3

The credit isn't taken. You only spend one when we return a result.

Does cold outreach actually work on these clients?5

Yes, but they're leads, not guaranteed clients. These are companies with a budget and stated intent, so reply rates are far better than untargeted cold email. Some buy, most don't — same as any pipeline.

What decides it is the pitch. Agencies getting the most out of this pick one thing they're excellent at and reach out only where they're clearly the best answer. A generic proposal sent to a well-targeted lead is still a generic proposal.

Why

Row 1 is the 50/50 disclosure. Written as a spec, not an apology. It belongs here, never in the hero — the mixed demo feed does the expectation-setting up top.

Row 4 is a conversion lever, not an FAQ answer. It also appears next to pricing.

This section used to be open with no accordions, and that was a deliberate rule. The old note read: "a collapsed objection is an unanswered one, and the whole point is that these are read without being clicked." It was overruled on space — five open answers ran 625px on desktop and 872px on a phone, which is most of a viewport of prose sitting between the product and the price. The fold is the concession; which row stays open is how the original concern gets paid for.

Row 1 stays open because nobody would click it. It's the 50/50 disclosure — a visitor doesn't arrive wondering whether they'll get a person or a company, so they'd never open it, and it's the one answer that stops the first paid reveal feeling like a bait-and-switch. Folding it would have been the one genuinely costly fold on the page.

Rows 2, 3 and 5 fold safely because they answer questions a visitor actually has. Someone worried about the data source, a ban, or whether cold outreach works arrives already holding that question; a visible question with an obvious affordance gets clicked. The question text does most of the reassurance work anyway — seeing "Can I get banned for contacting people directly?" printed on the page tells a careful buyer the concern is acknowledged before they read a word of the answer.

Row 4 folds for free. Its answer is deliberately duplicated next to pricing, so a visitor who never opens it here still meets "no result, no credit spent" further down. It's the only row on the page with that property, which is exactly why it can afford to be closed.

All five answers are now written — these were the last grey bars on the page. Rows 2 and 3 still need the lawyer before publishing, though; being written is not the same as being cleared.

Rows 2 and 3 are the two that get screenshotted. One says where the data comes from, the other says whether using it is safe. They're the reason a careful buyer either signs up or closes the tab, and they're the two most likely to be quoted back at you — so they stay plain, specific and short. No hedging language, no legal register, no "we take privacy seriously".

"Published openly" is not the same as "lawful to process". Under GDPR, personal data that someone published is still personal data, and row 2 doesn't settle the posture — it describes the sourcing honestly, which is the right thing for a landing page to do and not a substitute for the legal review. If counsel needs a sentence added, it goes here rather than into a policy nobody opens.

"Reply rates are far better than untargeted cold email" is a performance claim. Not comparative against a named company, so it doesn't carry the exposure the headline rules were guarding against — but it's still a claim you'd need to stand behind. Either get a number from the two customers and say it, or soften "far" before this ships.

Row 3's real defence is its last clause — the outreach happens somewhere else entirely. That's the load-bearing sentence in the answer, and it should survive any edit.

Build notes

  1. 1 Row 1 is open on load and the other four are closed. That split is the whole design of this section — see the margin for why each row falls the way it does. Don't "tidy" it by closing all five or opening all five; both are worse than the mix, in opposite directions.
  2. 6 The affordance has to be unmistakable, because the fold only works if people click. A row that looks like static text is a lost answer. Whole summary row is the hit target, not just the sign — and on mobile that row needs at least 44px of height. The + / – is a placeholder for whatever the designer picks; a chevron is fine, a mystery-meat dot is not.
  3. 6 Built on native <details>, and it should stay that way. No JavaScript, keyboard-operable and screen-reader-announced for free, and it works before the script runs — which is a rule on this page. A hand-rolled accordion would cost bytes and re-implement four accessibility behaviours the browser already has right.
  4. 6 Multiple rows can be open at once, deliberately. An exclusive accordion (which <details name> would give free) would slam row 1 shut the moment someone opens row 2, and row 1 is the one that must stay readable. It also stops anyone comparing two answers.
  5. 6 Expanding must not animate. Height transitions on open are the single most common accordion tell, and this page has a three-item motion budget that this is not on. Instant open, no easing, nothing to respect prefers-reduced-motion about.
  6. 6 Never collapse a row that's carrying a legal answer once counsel has signed it off. If the lawyer's wording ends up in row 2 or 3, check that "visible only after a click" is still acceptable to them — a disclosure behind an interaction is a different thing from a disclosure on the page, and that's their call, not a design one.
  7. 2 Flat, unbothered tone. These read as spec, not as reassurance. No warning icons, no tinted "trust" panel, no shield graphics — defensive design makes the answers look defensive.
  8. 3 This row appears twice. Here, and next to pricing. Same wording both times; it's a conversion lever, not an FAQ answer. If the copy drifts between the two, the guarantee stops sounding like a rule.
  9. 4 The honest answer is the short one. It declines to speak for someone else's terms, says exactly what is handed over, and points out that the outreach happens off that platform. Don't let this grow: every additional sentence of reassurance makes it read more like a disclaimer and less like a fact, and a long answer here is itself an admission that there's something to explain.
  10. 5 Two paragraphs, and the second one is the useful half. The first sets expectations — leads, not clients. The second tells them how to actually win, which is the paragraph that earns trust, because a page that admits a generic pitch will fail is a page that isn't selling magic. Keep them as separate paragraphs; run together, the qualification swallows the advice.
  11. — Objections is still the only place on the page where prose may run. Everywhere else is fragments and structure — don't let that leak upward into §2 or §3. Folding changed how much of it is visible at once, not the licence to write it.
  12. — Print and Cmd-F. Collapsed <details> content is invisible to print stylesheets and, in some browsers, to in-page search. Neither matters much for a landing page, but if anyone ever asks why an answer "isn't on the page", this is why.

Why

Advanced is the target, Pro is the decoy. $20 more than Pro buys 50% more reveals, 3× the scanners and the full archive. Both your current customers took that jump. Pro is now on the page rather than hidden in the compare table — a decoy only works if it's seen, and the $79→$99 step is the single most persuasive comparison in the section. Cost: four columns instead of three, so every card is narrower and the copy has to be shorter.

Free sits above the paid tiers, not beside them. It's the primary CTA now, not a lesser plan competing for the same click.

The per-reveal line does the saving argument. $1.30 → $0.79 → $0.66 → $0.50 reads left to right on its own. No percentage badges, no "save 24%" — nobody converts on a percentage.

Overage always costs more than the next tier's included rate. Starter overage is $2.00; on Advanced those reveals are $0.66. Run out twice and upgrading is arithmetic, not persuasion.

"1 reveal", never "1 contact". At a 50/50 person-to-company split, "contact" is a promise you'd break every second time.

Weekly refresh stated in plain sight and framed as freshness. Hiding it in fine print turns into refund requests.

Monthly billing changes the charge, never the credits. 10% off for paying four weeks up front; reveals still land every Monday at the same weekly number. Keeping the credit cadence weekly in both modes protects the freshness argument — "600 reveals a month" would invite people to save them up, and a three-week-old posting is worthless.

10% is the discount because it's the largest number that stays boring. It buys a month of runway per customer without teaching anyone to wait for a sale, and the per-reveal ladder still descends cleanly in monthly mode: $1.17 → $0.71 → $0.59 → $0.45.

The ROI block undercuts Advanced. 300 reveals a month fits inside Pro's 400 with room to spare, at $284 rather than $356. If the typical month really is 300, Advanced has to be sold on scanners, destinations and the full archive — not on reveal volume — or the example needs to be a heavier user. Worth resolving before this section is written, because right now the page argues against its own target plan.

Two blockers before this goes live. Pull both customers' actual weekly usage — Advanced was unlimited when they bought it, and capping it at 150 is a change to a live plan. Grandfather them on unlimited permanently; it costs nothing you currently collect and buys two references with no reason to be annoyed.

Funnel numbers are still assumptions, not measurements — 10% reply, 1% won, $3–5k a project are plausible agency figures, not yours. They're the most persuasive thing on the page once they're real, and the most damaging if a customer checks them.

  1. 1 A band, not a fourth card. Free is the primary CTA, so it must not sit in the row competing for the same glance as the paid plans. Full width above them, visually distinct, still clearly part of the ladder.
  2. 2 One marked plan only. Advanced carries the border and the flag. Resist marking a second — two highlights is no highlight, and this whole section is built so one plan wins.
  3. — Price baselines must align across all four cards. Right now they don't: the flag pushes Advanced's price down, and Agency's longer unit line pushes its bullets down. With three cards that read as looseness; with four it reads as broken. Solve it once — flag overlapping the top border, or the same reserved height on every card — and the descending price ladder becomes readable in one horizontal sweep, which is the entire mechanism of this row.
  4. — Four cards is the ceiling, and 720–900px is where it hurts. At the narrow end of the web layout the cards are about 150px wide and the feature bullets wrap to three lines. Don't add a third breakpoint to fix it — shorten the bullets. Every one of them should survive at 150px on two lines.
  5. — Pro sits immediately left of Advanced, and stays plain. Its whole job is to be read a half-second before Advanced and lose. No flag, no border, no "good value" label — the moment Pro looks appealing it stops working. Adjacency is the mechanism, so never reorder the row.
  6. 3 The per-reveal line is the savings argument. It has to be readable at a glance across all three cards so the descending price is visible without reading. No percentage badges, no "save 24%", no strike-through prices.
  7. 4 Sits in the price slot, same size as a number. "Talk to us" replaces a price, so it takes the price's position and weight. Don't shrink it into a link or the column stops reading as a tier.
  8. — Mobile reorders the plans: Advanced, Pro, Starter, Agency. On web the eye scans left to right and settles just right of centre; on mobile it reads top-down and the first card takes the attention. Source order would hand that to Starter — the one plan you don't want chosen. Pro follows Advanced so the comparison survives the stack, just reversed.
  9. 5 Closed by default. All four paid plans are already on the page, so this is for the one visitor in twenty who wants the Free column, the webhook row and the archive depths side by side. Expands in place, no scroll jump — and on mobile the matrix scrolls horizontally with the first column pinned, or it's unusable.
  10. 6 Highest-contrast element in the section. This single line removes the biggest objection to a credit model. It outranks every feature bullet around it.
  11. 7 Weekly is the default and stays selected. Weekly is the lower number and the smaller commitment, so it's what an undecided visitor should meet first; monthly is the upgrade they choose. Two segments, no third — annual is not on the table while the archive is four months deep on some sources. Switching must repaint the cards, the comparison matrix and the ROI block together and instantly; a price that animates between values reads as a slot machine. Design the pressed state as a real state, not a hover — people re-check which one they're looking at.
  12. 7 Never a struck-through weekly price next to the monthly one. Same rule as the per-reveal line: the "−10%" on the segment is the entire discount signal. No badges, no "save $40", no countdown.
  13. 8 The meter is the argument, the rows are the evidence. One dark sliver on a light track, drawn to scale against the low end of the range — the point is that the cost is visibly a rounding error, and it makes that point without a percentage anywhere. Square ends and greyscale, same as everything else; no gradient, no glow, no count-up. It does not animate — it is not one of the three moving things.
  14. 8 Ranges need two columns, not a wrap. "$3,000 – $5,000" and "22× – 37×" must sit on one line at every width, in tabular figures, or the block stops reading as arithmetic. On mobile the labels shorten before the numbers do.

Last step — motion, feel, and the speed ceiling

Sequence: motion is a polish pass

Design the static page and all four states first — card, type scale, spacing system. Only then spend the motion budget.

If the static page needs animation to feel good, the animation is compensating for something, and the page will feel expensive for two seconds and thin afterwards.

The speed ceiling comes first

This page has to load fast on a phone on mobile data. Speed is not a constraint on the premium feel — for an infrastructure product it is the premium feel.

  • No video, no Lottie, no WebGL, no scroll libraries
  • Hound as SVG, not a raster illustration
  • Two font weights maximum, self-hosted and subset
  • Animate transform and opacity only — nothing that triggers layout
  • If a decision trades load time for polish, take the load time

Where premium actually comes from

Not from effects. From typography with real weight range, a two-colour palette used confidently, a strict spacing scale, and craft in the small things — designed focus rings, optical alignment, tabular figures so the counter doesn't jitter.

Density done calmly. Cheap B2B is sparse and shouty; expensive B2B is dense and quiet. One elevated surface — the lead card — and everything else flat.

The hound has to be a crest, not a cartoon

A mascot and a $99/week price pull against each other. Draw it like a heraldic mark or an old hunting-club emblem: confident line, no big eyes, no smile, no speech bubbles. If it reads as a sticker, it costs more credibility than it buys warmth.

Motion budget — three things, total

  • Keyword cycle. Mechanical and confident. Hard swap or fast crossfade, never a slide-up. Holds while the pointer or focus is in the feed, and stops permanently on first interaction.
  • The reveal. The product's whole thesis in one interaction — this gets the craft. Mask lifts, card expands, content below moves aside. Under 400ms end to end. The hound-on-a-scent loading state lives here and nowhere else.
  • New lead arriving. One card slides in at the top while they watch. Proves "live" better than any label. Rate-limit hard — one arrival per ~20s, not a stream.

Explicitly ruled out

  • Scroll-triggered section entrances
  • Counters that animate up on scroll
  • Parallax of any kind
  • Card hover lifts and shadow transitions
  • Animated gradient backgrounds, cursor followers

Each of these ships in every template. Using them is how a page announces where it came from. And all of them cost load time for nothing.

Non-negotiables

  • Everything respects prefers-reduced-motion
  • Nothing loops forever except the keyword cycle, which holds on hover and stops on interaction
  • No animation blocks the demo — a visitor can type a keyword and hit Reveal before any of it finishes
  • The page is usable and legible before JavaScript runs

Name three products whose feel you want — not competitors, anything. Designers calibrate off references far faster than off adjectives, and "premium" means five different things to five people. One line, saves a round of revisions.
